\date{October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Language-model agents now carry out multi-hour data analyses, and on research benchmarks their answers are
often wrong. We study why with two methods. First, we traced 104 failed outputs from 16 runs of two computational biology
benchmarks, produced by three models, to the step in each transcript where the error entered. Second, we replayed 13 of
those steps with Claude Haiku 5.5 under eleven conditions. In 72\% of the failed GeneBench-Pro answers, the agent had itself
printed a number that contradicted the answer it later gave. In five case studies, the methodological fact that would
have prevented the failure was stated correctly when the model was asked directly, outside the task. At the replayed
steps, more reasoning effort, a generic reminder, a self-review prompt and five variants of a fresh reader did not raise
the rate at which the agent followed up the contradicting evidence above the baseline interval. A note that only pointed to
the relevant printed output, without saying what was wrong with it, did raise that rate, and recovered most of the effect
of a note that stated the question outright. The evidence is consistent with a bottleneck in which of its own results an
agent examines, rather than in what it knows.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Agents built on language models can now run long data analyses: they load files, write and execute code over many steps,
and report a result. Benchmarks of such agents on scientific tasks report low end-to-end success
\citep{chen2025scienceagentbench,majumder2025discoverybench,mitchener2025bixbench,koch2026bixbench3}. An end-to-end score
does not say what went wrong. This paper examines the transcripts of failed runs and asks a narrower question: at the step
where a failed analysis went wrong, what did the agent have in front of it, and what would have changed its next action?

One run illustrates the pattern we study. A DeepSeek V4.1 Flash agent estimating a carrier frequency in a GeneBench-Pro
problem ran a finer stratification as a robustness check. The check printed 0.2337. The agent's reported estimate was
0.1553. The agent's next statement was ``Everything checks out'', and it reported the original estimate. Two estimates of
the same quantity that differ by half are, in most analyses, the most informative result available; here the difference
was neither explained nor reported.

We find that this pattern is common and that it is not explained by missing knowledge. Our results are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Among 58 failed GeneBench-Pro answer keys, the agent had printed a number contradicting its final answer in 42
(72\%). Across 104 failed outputs on two benchmarks, the failures we attribute to the agent's analysis fall into three
classes: a default method applied without a check, an assumption checked only in aggregate, and printed disproving
evidence that was not acted on (\sref{sec:taxonomy}).
\item In five case studies, the fact that would have prevented the failure was stated correctly when the model was asked
directly, and was not applied in the run (\sref{sec:knowing}).
\item At 13 replayed failure steps, nine interventions that do not use knowledge of the answer, including maximum reasoning
effort and five kinds of fresh reader, did not raise the follow-up rate above the baseline interval. A note pointing to the
relevant printed output, with no content about it, raised the rate from 10 of 78 to 51 of 78 samples (\sref{sec:interventions}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A transcript-level classification of 104 benchmark failures with two independent
classification passes and a reported agreement statistic. (ii) Case studies contrasting what models state when asked
directly with what they do in the run. (iii) A controlled replay study at 13 failure steps that separates pointing an agent
to evidence from telling it what the evidence means. (iv) A set of recurring self-checking behaviours, each documented as a
case study with counts. The study covers three models in one agent harness on two benchmarks, with replays on one model; it
is not a comparison of models or harnesses.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Agents for data-driven science.} ScienceAgentBench \citep{chen2025scienceagentbench} and DiscoveryBench
\citep{majumder2025discoverybench} evaluate agents on tasks derived from published analyses. BixBench
\citep{mitchener2025bixbench} and BixBench3 \citep{koch2026bixbench3} target computational biology, the latter at the scale
of complete studies from raw data. Systems such as The AI Scientist \citep{lu2024aiscientist} and Biomni
\citep{huang2025biomni} automate larger parts of the research loop. These works report outcome accuracy; we examine the
decisions inside failed runs.

\paragraph{Failure taxonomies for agents.} \citet{cemri2025mast} build a taxonomy of failures in multi-agent systems from
annotated traces, organised around system design, inter-agent coordination and verification. Our classification is
narrower: single analysis decisions in long data-analysis runs, classified by what the agent had printed and read at the
step where the error entered.

\paragraph{Self-correction and critique.} Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} and Reflexion \citep{shinn2023reflexion}
improve outputs through the model's own feedback, and CRITIC \citep{gou2024critic} adds feedback from external tools.
\citet{huang2024selfcorrect} find that intrinsic self-correction without external feedback does not reliably improve
reasoning. Process supervision \citep{lightman2023verify} rewards correct intermediate steps. Our self-review and fresh-reader
conditions belong to this family. Their null effect at our failure steps is consistent with \citet{huang2024selfcorrect}.

\paragraph{Knowledge that is not expressed.} Models carry information about the correctness of their own answers
\citep{kadavath2022language}, internal representations encode truthfulness that outputs do not always reflect
\citep{burns2023latent,orgad2025know}, and information in the middle of a long context is used less well than information
near its ends \citep{liu2024lost}. We report a behavioural analogue: methodological facts stated when asked directly, and
not used at the step where they would have changed the analysis.

\paragraph{Errors in human analysis.} Three long-studied human failure modes have close counterparts in our data: testing a
hypothesis only with cases that would confirm it \citep{wason1960}, technical factors aligned with the variable of interest
\citep{leek2010}, and pooled associations that differ within groups \citep{simpson1951}. We cite them where a finding maps to
one of them.

\figref{fig:design} summarises the three parts of the study.

\subsection{Agent and runs}
All runs used one agent harness, in which a model writes and executes code in a sandbox against staged data files, can write
a plan, can delegate work to sub-agents, can consult method documentation, and receives short notes at turn boundaries. The
harness imposes no limit on the number of steps or turns. Before attributing any behaviour to a model we audited the
harness for confounds (output limits, context caching, instructions that discourage additional analysis, and matching of
outputs during evaluation) and corrected the issues found before running any replay. The classes checked are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:confounds}.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\textbf{GeneBench-Pro} public case studies: 10 synthetic genomics problems with 30 graded answer keys and tight tolerances.
Each problem is constructed so that a standard analysis gives a wrong answer unless a specific property of the data is
noticed. \textbf{BixBench3} \citep{koch2026bixbench3}: reproductions of published computational biology analyses from raw
data, each output graded against the published artifact. We analyse the 13 of its 20 tasks for which we had a graded run,
and count an output as failed when its score is below 0.8. \textbf{Our probe set}: problems we constructed outside biology
(an electrical-load time series, a repeated survey, a reaction-kinetics series) with known defects. We use it only for the
probes in \sref{sec:knowing} and the reasoning-effort study in \sref{sec:effort}, never to evaluate an intervention.

\subsection{Models}
The 16 benchmark runs comprise 13 BixBench3 task runs, with GPT-6.1 Sol as the root agent and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash as the
sub-agent model to which most execution was delegated, and 3 complete GeneBench-Pro runs of 10 problems each: two with
DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (trials 2 and 3) and one with GPT-6 Astra. Because the BixBench3 root agent delegated execution,
failures in those runs are attributed to the GPT-6.1 Sol root with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash sub-agents, not to either model alone.
All replays use Claude Haiku 5.5 with the API's reasoning effort set to high and adaptive thinking. Turns are scored by Claude
Sonnet 5.5; a sample is re-scored by Claude Opus 5.5 to measure agreement. Each probe names its model.

\subsection{Tracing failures}\label{sec:tracing}
For each failed output we reproduced the reference value, quantified the difference, and identified the smallest change to
the agent's own pipeline that removes it. We then read the transcript back to the step where that change should have been
made and recorded what the agent had printed and read up to that point. Each failure received one primary cause and, where
present, a contributing cause, from the classes in \figref{fig:taxonomy}. Causes were labelled established when the
smallest change was reproduced numerically, and probable otherwise (56 and 48 failures). One BixBench3 output was scored
from an intermediate file rather than the delivered one; it is retained and classed under tooling.

A second, independent pass rebuilt the classification from the same evidence. The two passes agree on 88 of 104 failures
(Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$); every disagreement concerns a failure for which two causes are of equal weight, and we report shares
under both passes. Failures within one problem often share a cause, so we also report shares over cause groups, defined as
the failures from one run of one problem that share a primary class (48 groups).

\subsection{Failure points and replays}\label{sec:replays}
A \emph{failure point} is the exact request the original agent sent to its model immediately after the disproving evidence
appeared in its context. We captured 13 failure points from runs by DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GPT-6 Astra and the GPT-6.1 Sol
root (\figref{fig:points}), and three points from runs that passed, which serve as controls. Each request was converted to
the Anthropic message format with its system prompt, conversation, tool calls, tool results and tool definitions; the
original model's private reasoning is not available and is not included. Claude Haiku 5.5 then produced the next turn in the
original agent's place. At baseline it follows up the disproving evidence in 13\% of samples, so the replay preserves the
failure.

Each condition changes one thing (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:conditions}), and each condition was sampled six times per point.
A turn \emph{follows up} the evidence when it names the disproving quantity and runs code to investigate it, or states
concretely what it changes; mentioning it while moving on does not count. The criterion for each point was written from the
tracing before any replay (Appendix~\ref{app:criteria}). At the control points, a turn counts as \emph{unwarranted rework}
when its only content re-checks a settled quantity or when it abandons a sound approach on a weak signal. The judge sees the
turn's text and code, never the condition. On a stratified random sample of 132 judged turns, 12 per condition, a second
model judge agreed with the first on 117 (88.6\%, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.73$; Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}). Both judges are
models; no human scoring was performed.

\subsection{Statistics}
We report counts. Pooled rates over the 13 points carry 95\% intervals from a bootstrap that resamples points (20{,}000
draws); with 13 clusters these intervals are approximate. Comparisons between two conditions use an exact two-sided sign test
on the points where they differ and an exact sign-flip permutation test on the per-point differences. We made nine comparisons
of non-answer-informed conditions against baseline without correction; no claim rests on any single one of them. Where a
count has fewer than ten samples we give it as $k$ of $n$ without an interval.

\label{fig:taxonomy}

\figref{fig:taxonomy} gives the primary causes. Between 31 and 38\% of failures, depending on the pass, arise where the
task's wording and the reference's convention disagree, for example on whether a class of reads is counted or which
annotation release defines the output (Appendix~\ref{app:conventions}). We set these aside, together with tooling failures
and one failure whose cause was not established. Of the remaining 60 to 70 failures, 53 to 60\% applied a default method where
the data needed a check first, 24 to 28\% held an assumption to be true in every unit of the data (each chromosome, each
marker) and checked it only in aggregate, and 16 to 18\% had a disproving number on screen as the primary cause. Over the 31
cause groups attributed to the agent's analysis, the corresponding shares are 58\%, 19\% and 23\%. State lost over runs of up
to 37 hours and output-format errors were not found as a primary cause.

The third class understates how often the evidence was present. Counting contributing causes, the agent had printed a number
contradicting its final answer in 42 of the 58 failed GeneBench-Pro keys (\figref{fig:taxonomy}, right). \tabref{tab:cases}
lists representative cases with the values the agents printed.

The errors also recur. On two problems, the two DeepSeek V4.1 Flash trials returned numerically identical wrong answers. On the
admixture-dating problem, both DeepSeek trials, the GPT-6 Astra run and a later Claude Haiku 5.5 run returned the same wrong pair
of estimates to two decimals. The error therefore recurs across models in the same harness and is not specific to one model's
weights. This design cannot separate the contribution of the harness from that of the models.

Two observations from these probes bear on mechanism. In the reaction-kinetics probe, the model wrote in its plan that the
activation energy's magnitude ``cannot be estimated'' before all temperatures were loaded, although it gives a tight typical

relate them. In both cases the knowledge was available when the question was posed and was not retrieved while the agent was
planning or executing.

\section{Interventions at failure points}\label{sec:interventions}

\subsection{Conditions}
We compared eleven conditions at each failure point (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Nine use no knowledge of the
answer: the baseline; maximum reasoning effort; a generic reminder (``is there anything in what you have computed so far that
you have not yet accounted for?''); a prompt asking the agent to pose and answer a reviewer's question about its last output;
and five fresh readers, separate model calls that see the task and the latest output and return one question (an open question,
a question about the analysis's assumptions, about the discrepancy with the largest effect on the answer, about disagreements
printed in the output, and all three of the last as a list). Two conditions use knowledge of the answer. A \emph{pointer} names
the relevant printed output by the agent's own label and says nothing about it, for example ``look again at the fine-cell reweight
partner line in the output you just got.'' An \emph{answer-informed question} states the question an analyst who knew the error
would ask, for example ``your finer-cell reweighting gave 0.2337 while your reported partner frequency is 0.1553: which one is
right, and which strata drive the difference?'' Both are written from the tracing and are measuring devices, not deployable
interventions: the answer-informed question is an upper bound on what any question-raising intervention could achieve at these
points, and the pointer is an upper bound on what selecting the right output alone could achieve.

\subsection{Results}
At baseline the agent followed up the disproving evidence in 10 of 78 samples (\figref{fig:interventions}). The nine conditions
that use no knowledge of the answer gave between 9 and 18 of 78, and every one of their intervals contains the baseline rate.
The best of them, the reader limited to printed disagreements, was above baseline at 5 points, below at 1 and tied at 7 (sign test
$p = 0.22$). None of these conditions produced unwarranted rework at the control points (0 of 18 each, which does not exclude
rates up to 18\%).

The pointer raised the follow-up rate to 51 of 78. It was above baseline at 12 of 13 points and tied at one (sign test and
permutation test, both $p = 0.0005$), and above the best fresh reader at 9 points, below at 1 and tied at 3 (sign test $p = 0.02$,
permutation test $p = 0.006$). The answer-informed question reached 67 of 78 and was above the pointer at 9 points, below at 2 and
tied at 2 (sign test $p = 0.07$, permutation test $p = 0.13$). Measured from baseline, the pointer recovered 72\% of the
answer-informed question's increase (41 of 57 samples). \figref{fig:points} gives the counts per point. The pointer was weakest
where the disproving quantity is not printed as a single value: at the QTL point it named an aggregate concordance (0 of 6), and at
the CRISPR-screen point a table of correlations (1 of 6).

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_points.pdf}

The fresh readers' questions were usually reasonable but concerned a different issue than the disproving one. At the Mendelian
randomization point (DeepSeek run) they asked about the definition of a standard deviation rather than the residual correlation;
at the admixture point, about the choice between two dating estimators rather than the per-chromosome fractions; at the read-counting
point, about an asymmetry between two conditions rather than the 587 loci with no counts. Each output carries several real
discrepancies, and a reader that sees only the output tends to select one that is not the error. When a reader did ask the disproving
question, at the treatment-effect point under the disagreement reader (6 of 6), the follow-up rate matched the answer-informed
question's.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Write the follow-up probability at point $m$ under condition $c$ as
\begin{equation}
\Pr(F_m \mid c) = s_c(m)\, u(m) + \bigl(1 - s_c(m)\bigr)\, b(m),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $s_c(m)$ is the probability that condition $c$ directs the agent to the disproving evidence, $u(m)$ the probability that it
follows up once directed, and $b(m)$ the probability that it follows up otherwise. The decomposition rests on an assumption we did
not test: that $u(m)$ does not depend on how the agent was directed. Under it, the answer-informed condition estimates $u$ at about
0.86, and the fresh readers' implied values of $s_c$ lie between 0 and 0.14, each with an interval that includes zero. The pointer
result constrains the interpretation without relying on the assumption. A note that carries no information about what is wrong,
only where to look, recovers most of the effect of one that states the problem. At these points the agent largely did the rest of
the work once its attention was on the right output. The evidence is therefore consistent with a bottleneck in which of its own
outputs the agent examines, rather than in what it knows or whether it acts. It does not show how to select that output without
knowing the answer, which is the open problem.

Two further measurements, on fewer samples, are in Appendix~\ref{app:multiturn}. Over six turns, all three readers' questions
delivered together helped where the evidence was a printed disagreement and not where it was an assumption held as given. A list
of checks written by a fresh model call at the agent's first look at the data, which named the decisive check for four of nine
problems, did not change the outcome when the runs were continued to the end (9 of 16 forks against 9 of 18).

\subsection{Reasoning effort on one problem}\label{sec:effort}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_effort.pdf}
\caption{Electrical-load time series from our probe set. Left: 15 Claude Haiku 5.5 forks per effort schedule, and 10 Claude Sonnet
5.5 forks, from the turn after the agent found a 28-row gap, continued to the end and graded by the problem's grader. Right: effort
high against maximum on the first reply at that turn (30 samples per level), on the graded outcome, and at the 13 failure points of
\figref{fig:interventions}. Median visible reasoning on the first reply: 761 characters at high, 1,579 at maximum.}
\label{fig:effort}
\end{figure}

In the load series, timestamps after one date are shifted by seven hours; the shift appears as 28 missing rows on that date and 28
rows past the end of the year, in the same hours. On this single problem the reasoning budget at the decision turn decided the
outcome (\figref{fig:effort}). With effort high throughout, 1 of 15 forks was graded correct; with maximum effort throughout, 12 of
15; with high effort on the first reply and maximum afterwards, 15 of 15. Claude Sonnet 5.5 at effort high passed 9 of 10. At high
effort, first replies took the task's statement that there is no daylight-saving change as settling the time base and computed the
answer; at maximum effort, all 30 replies named the coincidence between the gap and the overhang and proposed several hypotheses
about the time base, each of which led to a check that split the data by period. At the 13 failure points, by contrast, maximum
effort gave 9 of 78 against 10 of 78. On this evidence, more reasoning helped on one problem where several hypotheses led to the same
partitioning check, and did not help at points where the agent already had several candidate issues in view.

\section{Recurring self-checking behaviours}\label{sec:checking}

This section documents six behaviours as case studies, each with the problems and models in which we observed it. Several were
observed in only one problem; the counts are small, and the behaviours are offered as descriptions, not estimates of prevalence.

\subsection{Checks that could not have failed}
A check is informative about an assumption to the extent that its result would differ if the assumption were false. Many checks in
the failed runs had no such power (\tabref{tab:checks}). In the repeated survey, a reversed wave maps each response $x$ on the 1 to 5
scale to $6 - x$. A range check passes either way. The correlation of the reversed item with the other items in that wave changes sign,
so a within-wave correlation check would have detected it; the agent stated both facts when asked directly (\tabref{tab:probes}) and ran
the range check. The other checks in \tabref{tab:checks} share the decision they were meant to test: a bootstrap resamples within the
chosen specification, and two pipeline variants that apply the same exclusion agree. This is the pattern \citet{wason1960} described
in people testing a rule only with cases that would confirm it.

\subsection{Predictions widened until they could not fail}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig7_predictions.pdf}
\caption{Left: target-gene counts per replicate predicted by GPT-6.1 Sol in two plans for BixBench3 task 1, the typical range it gives
when asked directly, and the agent's own result. Right: the per-5\,$^\circ$C rate ratio GPT-6.1 Sol gives when asked directly, against
the 87-fold jump in the reaction-kinetics probe's data. Three plans or samples per wording.}
\label{fig:predictions}
\end{figure}

A prediction stated before computing is what makes a result surprising. Asked in a plan for the expected result of each step, GPT-6.1
Sol gave the output's shape (``85 columns, 1,144 rows''). Asked for a magnitude, it gave target-gene counts of 1,000 to 20,000 and 100
to 20,000 and called each ``deliberately broad''; the agent's result, 3,594 to 6,904, lies inside both (\figref{fig:predictions}). Asked
for a narrow prediction, it gave only quantities fixed by the task, such as counts of columns and degrees of freedom. Across five wordings
of the request, three plans each, no plan stated a prediction that the defect could have contradicted. The same request made at the start
of the step itself, to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash sub-agents executing the step, produced tight typical ranges (3 of 3 samples) and, in 9 of 9
samples, a description of what the output would look like if the data carried no signal.

\subsection{Lenient comparison with one's own prediction}
When a sub-agent's own prediction was returned to it beside its result, it judged agreement leniently. With a prediction of $10^2$ to $10^3$
peaks per replicate and a result of 1,605 to 1,747, all three samples described the result as within or at the upper end of the
prediction (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash sub-agent, BixBench3 task 1). With a narrower prediction of tens to a few hundred, two of three samples
said the result was an order of magnitude above it; asked to test reproducibility where the numbers disagreed, two of three then cited the
looser of two bounds in their own note and passed the result. The returned prediction did reliably cause the agent to compute the check it
had named (5 of 6 and 6 of 6 samples in two rounds). The comparison was lenient; the computation was not.

\subsection{Disagreeing estimates reported as a range}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig6_timing.pdf}
\caption{Twelve failed GeneBench-Pro runs, each from its first transcript step to its answer (step count at right), with the steps at which
the agent printed the disproving number (\tabref{tab:cases}). Step indices were located by searching each transcript for the printed value.}
\label{fig:timing}
\end{figure}

When two of its own estimates of one quantity disagreed, the agent tended to report a span and keep the first estimate. In DeepSeek V4.1
Flash trial 3, the partner frequency was reported as ``0.117 to 0.146 across alternative weightings'', omitting the 0.236 printed in the same
table; the single-cell analysis listed variants that omitted the printed $-0.62$ and $-0.55$; and the treatment-effect analysis chose the
sparsest specification by an information criterion over four printed alternatives and reported the estimate as ``robust across model
specifications''. In all three cases the omitted
estimate was closer to the reference value. These runs did not run out of steps. In the twelve runs of \figref{fig:timing}, the first print of
the disproving number came with a median of 59\% of the run's steps still ahead (range 31 to 82\%).

\subsection{A pooled estimate absorbed the unit that disagreed}
When an assumption fails in one unit of several, a pooled estimate absorbs the failure. At the admixture point, Claude Haiku 5.5 printed the
ancestry fraction of each of two haplotypes on each of four chromosomes (0.79, 0.49, 0.21, 0.81 and 0.55, 0.02, 0.88, 0.41). Treating
``sampling noise'' as a hypothesis gives a test the agent could have run on that table. If the labels are inverted on one chromosome,
relabelling that chromosome should reduce the spread across chromosomes in both haplotypes at once; under noise, no single relabelling should
stand out. Of the four single-chromosome relabellings, only that of the third chromosome reduces the summed standard deviation, from 0.640 to
0.401; the others leave it at 0.55 or above. With four units the test is weak, but it is one computation on a table the agent had already
printed. Told to compute the table, Claude Haiku 5.5 did so in 10 of 10 forks and kept the pooled answer in 10 of 10, describing the spread as
``real heterogeneity''. The pattern resembles the reversal of pooled associations within groups described by \citet{simpson1951}.

\subsection{Descriptions of good practice did not trigger checks}
Text describing good practice did not change behaviour at the moments it addressed. Removing instructions that discouraged unrequested
diagnostics did not change the follow-up rate at failure points from the original runs (1 of 15 against 3 of 15, original models). A sentence
encouraging tests raised the share of first replies that split the load profile by period from 9 to 17 of 30 but left the graded outcome at 1
of 15 (Claude Haiku 5.5). In one admixture-dating run (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, trial 3), a method document in the agent's context stated that a
date driven by one chromosome should be treated as a warning; the agent printed the per-chromosome values and did not compare them.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The following account is offered as a hypothesis consistent with the measurements, not as an established mechanism. During a run, agents
appear to treat computed results as outputs to report rather than as observations to interpret: a printed number that contradicts the headline
is filed, not investigated. The checks an agent chooses are generated from its current model of the data, so they vary that model's
parameters and rarely test its premises (\tabref{tab:checks}). Predictions made while planning are widened until they cannot be contradicted,
which removes the expectation against which a result would be surprising (\figref{fig:predictions}). An adequate explanation, such as
``noise'' or ``real heterogeneity'', ends the inquiry. The intervention results fit this account: content about the error was largely
unnecessary, and direction to the relevant output was largely sufficient (\figref{fig:interventions}).

For the design of research agents, the results suggest that the scarce operation is choosing which of the agent's own outputs to re-examine.
Generic reminders, more reasoning, self-review and fresh readers that see only the latest output did not perform that choice at our failure
points. Approaches that let the agent record an expected result before computing, and return that record beside the result, address part of
the problem; our measurements show that the agent computes the check it recorded, but also that its recorded predictions are often too wide to
be contradicted. Whether such mechanisms close the gap measured here, without causing rework on sound analyses, requires evaluation on problems
not used to design them.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The failure points are in-sample.} All 13 points were studied before the conditions were designed. The pointer and the
answer-informed question were written with knowledge of the error, so they bound what selection and full guidance could achieve; neither is a
method. No condition was evaluated on held-out problems.
\item \textbf{The pointer was not run at the control points.} Its rate of unwarranted rework on sound analyses is unknown, and a pointer to an
arbitrary output in a sound analysis may cause rework.
\item \textbf{Replays put one model in another's context.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answers in place of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1
Sol agents, without their private reasoning. Haiku also usually fails to follow up at baseline (13\%), so the replay preserves the failure, but it
is not the original agent.
\item \textbf{One turn is short.} Most replays measure the next turn only; the multi-turn and early-list forks cover four and nine problems.
\item \textbf{Scoring and classification are by models.} Two model judges agree on 88.6\% of a stratified sample ($\kappa = 0.73$), and two
classification passes agree on 85\% of failures ($\kappa = 0.79$). Neither was validated against human annotation, and under the answer-informed
condition the agent's reply often quotes the note, which can reveal the condition to the judge.
\item \textbf{Small samples.} Most cells hold 3 to 6 samples, and the pooled rates rest on 13 points; bootstrap intervals with 13 clusters are
approximate. The knowledge probes use 3 to 30 samples each and different models on the two sides of some rows.
\item \textbf{Scope.} Three models, one harness, two benchmarks concentrated in genomics, and synthetic problems in GeneBench-Pro. Harness and
model effects are not separable in this design.
\item \textbf{Classification depends on reading.} Shares move by up to ten percentage points between the two passes; we report both.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In 104 failures of long data analyses by three models on two benchmarks, the agent had often already printed the number that contradicted its
answer, and the relevant methodological facts were available when the models were asked directly. At 13 replayed failure steps, interventions
that did not use knowledge of the answer did not change behaviour, while a note that only pointed to the relevant output recovered most of the
effect of stating the problem. These results locate a measurable gap between what the agents know and what they examine. Closing it requires a
way to select the output worth re-examining without knowing the answer, evaluated on problems not used to design it.

\section{Follow-up criteria}\label{app:criteria}
Fixed before any replay. Admixture dating: compares the ancestry fraction per haplotype per chromosome, or questions whether labels are
consistent across chromosomes. Chromatin-contact loop strength: investigates the case-only block of high pixels or the relation of coverage to
GC content, or excludes the block from the background model. Mendelian randomization, residual correlation: treats the permutation result as a
sign that the instrument model is wrong. Mendelian randomization, heterogeneity: treats $Q$ as evidence that the model is wrong and finds which
instruments drive it. QTL mapping: computes held-out concordance per marker. Carrier frequency: names both estimates and works out which is right.
Read counting: asks which loci have no counts and why. Treatment effect: asks why adjusting for the covariate moves the estimate.
Structural-variant association: asks why the adjustment moves the estimate. Gene Ontology summary: treats the root-only genes as a possible defect
in its own lookup and tests it. Single-cell expression QTL: tests contamination by genotype beyond an additive term. CRISPR interference screen:
tests dependence on GC content beyond a linear term, or by plate, among the guides where GC tracks knockdown.

\section{Confound audit}\label{app:confounds}
Before any replay we checked, and where needed corrected, the following classes of harness effect: limits on model output length, including
whether reasoning tokens count against them; prompt-cache behaviour and token accounting; whether reasoning content is carried between turns;
instructions in the system prompt or in delegation briefs that discourage additional analysis; whether a failed or interrupted code cell is
reported to the agent; and how evaluation matches delivered files to required outputs.

\section{Disagreements between task wording and reference convention}\label{app:conventions}
Examples, without answer values. BixBench3 task 12: the task instructs that reads mapping to multiple genomic locations are not counted; the
reference counts at multi-copy loci are consistent with such reads having been counted. BixBench3 task 10: the output universe follows an
annotation release; the task names no release, and the reference corresponds to an earlier release than the one retrieved at run time.
GeneBench-Pro structural-variant association: equally defensible constructions of the dosage variable give estimates spread over a range wider than
the grading tolerance. GeneBench-Pro chromatin-contact loop strength: the control estimate varies across defensible specifications of the expected
background by more than the tolerance.

\section{Agreement}\label{app:agreement}
\textbf{Classification.} The two passes assign the same primary class to 88 of 104 failures (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.79$). The 16 disagreements are
failures whose tracing names two causes of equal weight: 11 assigned to the default-method class by one pass and to the wording-and-convention class
by the other, 4 assigned to the wording-and-convention class by one pass and to tooling by the other, and 1 left unattributed by one pass.
\textbf{Judging.} On 132 turns sampled at random, 12 from each condition, the second judge agrees with the first on 117 (88.6\%, $\kappa = 0.73$). The
first judge scored 39 of the 132 as following up and the second 42; per condition the two counts differ by at most two. On the 12 answer-informed turns
the judges agree on 10.
